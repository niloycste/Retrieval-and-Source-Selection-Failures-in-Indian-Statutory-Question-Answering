\title{Beyond Answer Extraction: Diagnosing Retrieval and Source Selection Failures in Indian
Statutory Question Answering}

% Author names and affiliations are supplied through the confidential
% submission fields and omitted from the double-anonymous manuscript.

%% ============================================================
\abstract{Answering a question about a statute requires finding a supporting
provision and extracting the relevant information. A model may extract an
answer well when the source is supplied, yet fail when it must first find and
choose that source. This study presents a stage-wise diagnostic framework that
separates candidate access, source selection, answer extraction, and assessed
legal adequacy. It applies the framework to IndicIPR-QA, a benchmark covering
seven Indian intellectual property Acts. With the reader and its
answer selection rule fixed, F1 falls from 87.37 with the annotated paragraph
to 31.95 when BM25 searches paragraph text alone. Adding statutory structure
through section titles and section filtering substantially improves recovery
of the annotated source. Controls examine the separate contributions of smaller
candidate pools and word matching. However, matching a section number does not
necessarily identify the intended Act, and source selection errors remain under
both the reader decoder and an independent reranker. An exploratory analysis
also links question wording with retrieval difficulty. A human audit finds
that agreement with the benchmark annotation does not necessarily match assessed
legal adequacy; it does not estimate legal error rates across the full test
set. Together, these findings show how separate measures of retrieval, source
selection, extraction, and human assessment help explain QA scores on this
benchmark. The findings apply to the tested questions and systems; they do not
establish a general limit on legal retrieval.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Statutory question answering requires a system to find a supporting provision
and extract the relevant information. Statutes are organised into Acts,
sections, subsections, and clauses, yet different provisions may use similar
words. A passage may therefore differ from the source intended by a benchmark
while remaining relevant under another legal interpretation. This reflects the
broader view that legal relevance depends on the task and intended use
\citep{van2017concept,vsavelka2022legal}.

When the annotated paragraph is supplied, a model only extracts the answer.
When the source must first be retrieved, the system must also find and select
that paragraph. The \emph{retrieval--reading gap} is the difference between
these settings. An overall score can hide whether the annotated paragraph was
not retrieved, another source was selected, or the extracted span did not
match the annotation. Separating these outcomes identifies the affected stage.

Some questions describe the needed information, while others mainly name a
section or clause. Descriptive words support content matching, whereas a
locator points to statutory structure and may say little about the answer.
Because section identifiers recur across Acts, a reference can narrow the
candidates without identifying the intended Act. The analysis examines how
these patterns relate to annotated-source recovery and later pipeline stages.

Throughout this paper, retrieval success means recovery of the
benchmark-annotated evidence paragraph. An \emph{annotated-source retrieval
failure} occurs when that paragraph is absent from the candidate pool. A
\emph{source selection failure} occurs when the paragraph is available but the
system chooses another one. An \emph{answer extraction failure} occurs when
the system chooses the annotated paragraph but predicts a span that does not exactly match the
annotated answer. These annotation-relative measures do not determine the
governing provision or exclude another legally relevant source. A separate
human audit examines legal adequacy in 80 sampled cases.

The analysis uses IndicIPR-QA, a benchmark of 4,462 extractive question and
answer pairs over seven Indian intellectual property Acts
\citep{anonymous2026indiciprqa}. The fixed LegalBERT--SQuAD--IPR reader performs
strongly when given the annotated paragraph but substantially worse when BM25
retrieves from paragraph text alone. This retrieval--reading gap motivates an
investigation of where pipeline errors arise and how they vary across questions.

With the reader fixed, the experiments compare paragraph-only and title-aware
retrieval, section matching, and changes to query references. Section filtering
keeps passages whose section identifier matches the question and ranks them
with BM25; controls separate title information, candidate-pool reduction, and
ranking. The main methodological contribution is a stage-wise diagnostic
framework for statutory QA. It separates candidate access, source selection,
answer extraction, and assessed legal adequacy while keeping the answer reader
fixed. The framework is used to examine how statutory structure affects
annotated-source recovery and which errors remain. Section filtering is one
diagnostic intervention within the framework, not a proposed optimal method.

The study addresses three research questions:

\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1: Retrieval.] How much does performance decrease when the system
    must retrieve the annotated paragraph instead of being given it?

    \item[RQ2: Question wording.] Are annotated-source retrieval failures more
    common in questions classified by the operational reference removal rule as
    a zero-residual reference pattern?

    \item[RQ3: Role of references.] How do statutory references in a query act
    as structural signals for retrieving the annotated paragraph?
\end{description}

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A stage-wise diagnostic framework separates annotated-source
    retrieval, source selection, answer extraction, and human assessment of
    legal adequacy. Holding the reader fixed makes it possible to trace how
    candidate changes propagate through the same QA pipeline.

    \item Several controlled tests examine the link between statutory
    references in the query, retrieval, and source selection. The comparison
    covers sparse, dense, fusion, and reranking systems, together with controls
    for titles, candidate-pool size, and ranking within a matched section pool.

    \item Query-reference interventions and an operational question grouping
    examine how locators and descriptive words contribute to retrieval. An
    80-case audit by two law graduate students then compares benchmark
    agreement with assessed source relevance and answer adequacy.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Legal question answering brings together two tasks: finding
relevant information and using it to produce an answer. The following review
covers the benchmarks used to study these tasks, methods for retrieving
statutory provisions, and approaches that use the structure of legal documents.
This provides the context for examining how references within questions affect
retrieval and the interpretation of answer scores.

Finding a legal source may require more than matching a topic. The result can
depend on legal authority, jurisdiction, and the structure of Acts, sections,
clauses, and cross-references. A passage with similar words may therefore be
unsuitable for the question. This makes the source and its place in the statute
important parts of retrieval evaluation
\citep{van2017concept,vsavelka2022legal}.

\subsection{Question Answering and Legal Benchmarks}

Extractive question answering asks a model to select an answer span from a
passage. SQuAD~1.1~\citep{rajpurkarSQuAD100000Questions2016} is a widely used
benchmark for this task and provides the reader transfer stage used here.
Later datasets added questions designed to appear answerable when they are
not, questions from search engines, automatically linked evidence, reasoning
across documents, and reading comprehension from examinations
\citep{rajpurkarKnowWhatYou2018,kwiatkowskiNaturalQuestionsBenchmark2019,
joshiTriviaQALargeScale2017,yangHotpotQADatasetDiverse2018,
laiRACELargescaleReAding2017}. These resources study different forms of
reading and reasoning rather than retrieval of statutory provisions.

Legal benchmarks target different documents and tasks. CUAD
\citep{hendrycksCUADExpertAnnotatedNLP2021} supports clause level review of
commercial contracts, while JEC-QA
\citep{zhongJECQALegalDomainQuestion2020} contains multiple choice questions
from China's National Judicial Examination. LegalBench contains 162 tasks
covering six types of legal reasoning; LexGLUE brings together several legal
language understanding datasets; and LawBench evaluates 20 Chinese legal tasks
at three cognitive levels
\citep{guha2023legalbench, chalkidisLexGLUEBenchmarkDataset2022,
feiLawBenchBenchmarkingLegal2024}. Resources focused on legislation bring source
texts more directly into QA. DiscoLQA uses discourse units for zero-shot QA
over European legislation, while EQUALS evaluates QA by reading Chinese laws
\citep{sovrano2025discolqa,chen2023equals}. IPBench evaluates broader
intellectual property knowledge and practice rather than verbatim answer span
extraction from Indian IPR statutes
\citep{wangIPBenchBenchmarkingKnowledge2025}.

Indian legal NLP has also studied judgment prediction and explanation, as
well as continued pretraining on Indian legal text
\citep{malikILDCCJPEIndian2021,paul2023pre}. AILQA evaluates
retrieval augmented generation for Indian legal QA
\citep{nigam2026ailqa}. INLegalLlama combines continued pretraining and
supervised fine-tuning for Indian judgment prediction
\citep{nigamNyayaAnumanaINLegalLlamaLargest2025}. The present study focuses on
a different task: retrieving the annotated statutory paragraph and extracting
an answer span from it. Rather than comparing generated answers, it holds the
reader fixed and attributes errors to candidate access, source selection, or
span extraction.

\subsection{Retrieving and Reranking Statutory Provisions}

Retrieval methods connect a question with relevant passages in different ways.
BM25~\citep{robertsonProbabilisticRelevanceFramework2009} uses lexical
matching, while dense passage retrieval
\citep{karpukhinDensePassageRetrieval2020} represents questions and passages
as vectors for semantic matching. BGE-M3 is a multilingual embedding model
that supports dense, sparse, and multiple vector retrieval; this study uses only
its dense representation \citep{chenM3Embedding2024}. Cross-encoders
\citep{gao2021rethinkbert} score questions and passages together and can
rerank candidates from an initial retrieval stage. Retrieval augmented
generation~\citep{lewisRetrievalAugmentedGenerationKnowledgeIntensive2020}
uses retrieved passages as evidence for answer generation. These methods show
that retrieval quality can affect the evidence available to the final answer.

For statutory retrieval, BSARD~\citep{louisSpanakisBSARD2022} introduced more
than 1,100 French language legal questions from Droits Quotidiens and more than
22,600 Belgian statutory articles. Experienced jurists linked the questions to
relevant articles. Its experiments showed that supervised dense retrieval can
increase recall. Building on this setting, \citet{louisFindingLaw2023} used a
graph augmented dense retriever to model relationships among statutory
articles. Their approach uses connections between provisions instead of
treating each article as an independent document.

Legal RAG benchmarks also evaluate retrieval together with downstream legal
answering. \citet{zhengReasoningFocusedLegalRetrieval2025} study bar exam and
housing statute questions, while \citet{afaneBenchmarkingLegalRAG2026} examine
statutory surveys. A closely related study by \citet{alshehri2026neural}
evaluates UK statutory retrieval using BM25 and dense candidate generation
followed by ten neural rerankers. It also distils a proprietary reranker into
an open ModernBERT cross-encoder. Its main focus is ranking relevant
provisions within the candidate set. The present study instead follows
retrieved candidates through a fixed answer reader and reports retrieval,
source selection, extraction, and assessed legal adequacy separately.

\subsection{Legal Structure and References in Questions}

Legal structure can provide useful information for retrieval. Alongside the
Belgian graph approach discussed above, JuriFindIT
\citep{noceJuriFindITItalian2026} represents references between Italian
statutory articles in a heterogeneous graph. For Thai legal QA,
\citet{akarajaradwongNitiBench2025} evaluate hierarchy aware chunking and
cross-reference handling. These studies use legal structure to organise and
retrieve evidence.

Other studies consider evidence spread across multiple provisions.
\citet{chaeStructureAwareStatuteQA2026} use graph guided retrieval to find
regulatory evidence across a document hierarchy and evaluate performance when
statutory context is incomplete. Citation prediction is a related but
different task. \citet{han2026legal} compare prompting, sparse and dense
retrieval, task specific fine-tuning, and reranking for predicting Australian
legal citations. Together, these studies show why legal relevance depends on
the task and cannot be judged from a shared topic alone
\citep{vsavelka2022legal,van2017concept}.

The closest studies mainly improve provision retrieval, model document-side
structure, rerank candidates, or evaluate retrieval within a RAG pipeline.
Those goals differ from attributing an answer error to candidate access,
source selection, or span extraction under a fixed reader. This study makes
that attribution the main object of evaluation. It also examines a structural
signal already present in the query: tests remove, isolate, or replace the
reference, while controls examine titles, candidate-pool size, and ranking.
The human audit adds a separate comparison between benchmark agreement and
assessed legal adequacy. The framework therefore complements work on stronger
retrievers, hierarchy-aware models, graphs, and legal RAG; it does not claim
to outperform those systems. Supervised retrieval and RAG systems may achieve
higher scores.

\section{Research Design: Benchmark, Tasks, and Evaluation}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

\subsection{Study Sequence}

RQ1 compares retrieval performance using the same reader, questions, and
corpus. RQ2 divides the questions into two groups based on their wording
after removing references and question templates. RQ3 tests the role of
statutory references by changing how they are used during retrieval.

After examining the results by Act, we inspected the test questions as an
exploratory step. The reference removal patterns, the list of question frame words, and the rule
that defines the two groups were then developed from this test material.
They were not developed on a separate set of questions.
Appendix~\ref{app:review_checks} gives the full 54-word list and the automatic
check of reference matching. The groups describe the result of a text rule;
they are not expert labels of question meaning. The reference tests were
defined before those experiments ran, but no plan was formally registered in
advance. RQ2 is therefore an exploratory characterization of patterns in this
benchmark, not a confirmatory test of a predefined question taxonomy. RQ3 is
also exploratory and was not preregistered. Neither analysis provides
independent confirmation or shows that the effects apply elsewhere. The RQ1
gap, the retrieval comparisons, the metadata controls, and the source
selection comparison can be computed without this question grouping rule.
They remain diagnostic measurements on the same fixed benchmark, rather than
an external test of generalization.

\subsection{IndicIPR-QA}\label{sec:dataset_description}

IndicIPR-QA is an extractive QA benchmark with 4,462 question--answer
pairs from 1,116 statutory paragraphs across the Copyright
Act, 1957; Patents Act, 1970; Trade Marks Act, 1999; Designs Act, 2000;
Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, 1999;
Semiconductor Integrated Circuits Layout-Design Act, 2000; and Protection of
Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act, 2001
\citep{anonymous2026indiciprqa}. Each answer is copied exactly from a continuous
span in its source paragraph. Questions were generated by a model, then
filtered and curated. The dataset is split by statute section into 3,710
training, 408 validation, and 344 test pairs. No supervised context is shared
across these splits. Dataset validation includes a two-annotator review of
200 pairs for question clarity, answer correctness, and answer minimality
\citep{anonymous2026indiciprqa}. Dataset construction and validation are
described in a resource paper accepted for publication at IEEE DSAA 2026; its
proceedings entry is forthcoming. Minimality concerns whether the answer contains
only the text needed to answer the question. The experiments use the released
splits without changing them. Access details are given in the Data and Code
Availability statement.

Two properties are important for this study. First, answers average 35.6 words,
so answer boundaries need careful handling. Mean answer length alone does not
show that this task is harder than other QA tasks. Second, questions identify
the target provision in different ways. Some describe its content, while others
mainly use a section or subsection reference. This distinction emerged from
the analysis. It was not defined in advance.

\subsection{Gold Context and Full Pipeline Evaluation}

Annotated context, or gold context, evaluation gives the model the annotated
paragraph and measures answer extraction. Full pipeline evaluation first retrieves
candidate paragraphs. The same reader then selects a source and extracts an
answer. Both evaluations use the same 344 test questions. Retrieval searches
all 1,116 corpus paragraphs.

The experiments use the fixed \fixedreader{} reader. It is based on
\texttt{nlpaueb/legal-bert-base-uncased}~\citep{chalkidis2020legalbert} and was
fine-tuned first on SQuAD~v1 and then on the IndicIPR-QA training split. It
reaches 87.37 F1 when given the annotated paragraph. The saved model and reader
settings stay fixed across retrieval experiments. This makes it possible to
relate changes in full pipeline scores to changes in the retrieved candidates.
The source selection counts therefore apply to this reader and decoder.

The reader was chosen after earlier results were available, from existing
reader configurations. It was not chosen using a validation set created for
this study. The study records do not contain a complete list of the alternatives,
so no total is reported. Keeping this reader fixed makes the comparisons
consistent; it does not establish that the reader is optimal. The same test
set was used for model choice, error analysis, and reference tests. The results
are therefore comparisons on the same questions within a fixed pipeline, not
a new evaluation on unseen data.

The full pipeline evaluation consists of three stages: candidate retrieval,
source selection, and answer extraction. Figure~\ref{fig:qa_pipeline}
illustrates these stages and the corresponding failure points.

\fixedreader{} predicts one continuous answer span by scoring possible start
and end positions. Long contexts are split into overlapping 384-token windows
with a stride of 128 tokens. Only valid spans where $s\leq e$ are considered. The
decoder selects the span with the highest summed start--end logit across all
windows. These scores are raw logits, the model scores before conversion to
probabilities. The main pipeline applies no local softmax or adjustment to make
scores comparable across passages. This rule selects a source but does not
give a calibrated estimate of its relevance. A passage with
more windows has more chances to receive a high score. The rule does not
correct for this effect. Appendix~\ref{app:review_checks} reports extra checks
for selection and answer length.
There is no further answer length limit, although each span must fit within
one context window. This avoids an arbitrary short answer cutoff in a corpus
with long annotated spans. It does not guarantee sensible boundaries.
The predicted answer is returned as the corresponding character span from the
source paragraph. Nineteen of the 344 test inputs require multiple windows; in 14
cases, the annotated answer is absent from the first window. The same decoder is
used for all main evaluation settings. In two complementary selector checks,
BGE-reranker-v2-m3 selects one passage from either the section-filter top-five
pool or the title-aware neural top-five pool. The fixed reader then extracts
only from that passage, so raw span logits do not choose the source in these
controls.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Normalised Exact Match (EM) and F1 at token level are the main metrics for answer text.
Before scoring, predicted and annotated answers are converted to lowercase.
The ASCII characters in Python's \texttt{string.punctuation} are removed, and
extra spaces are normalised. Articles remain. This differs from the official
SQuAD normalisation. Removing punctuation makes the score insensitive to some
legally important boundary differences. It does not remove those differences
from the source text or predicted character span. EM measures whether the
normalised prediction exactly matches the reference:
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{EM}=\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{norm}(\hat{a})=\mathrm{norm}(a)\},
	\label{eq:em}
\end{equation}
where $a$ and $\hat{a}$ denote the annotated and predicted answer strings,
respectively.

For F1 at token level, let $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{P}$ denote the annotated and
predicted token multisets. Precision, recall, and F1 are computed as
\begin{align}
	\mathrm{Precision} &= \frac{|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{P}|}{|\mathcal{P}|}, &
	\mathrm{Recall}  &= \frac{|\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{P}|}{|\mathcal{A}|}, \\
	\mathrm{F1}   &= \frac{2\,\mathrm{Precision}\,\mathrm{Recall}}
	{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
	\label{eq:f1}
\end{align}
If either token multiset is empty, the implementation assigns an F1 score of
zero.

EM requires an exact match after normalisation. F1 at token level gives partial
credit for overlapping tokens. Neither metric shows whether the answer came
from the annotated paragraph. The evaluation therefore also reports how often
that source is selected, both overall and among questions where it is in the
top five. EM/F1 grounded in the annotation is zero when the system
selects another paragraph. Grounded EM equals the exact match count from the
annotated source in Table~\ref{tab:stage_decomposition} divided by 344. These
grounding measures use benchmark annotations. They are not expert judgements that the
annotated paragraph is the only correct legal source. We do not use
character frequency overlap because it ignores character order and can give
high scores to legally different spans. Appendix~\ref{app:secondary} documents
the older artifact field that stores this measure.

\subsection{Human Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:human_protocol}

The human audit examines the section-filter top-five pipeline with the original
raw logit decoder. A random sample with seed 42 selects 20 questions without replacement
from each of its four outcomes: annotated source absent, available but not
selected, selected without exact match, and selected with exact match. The
corresponding test-set populations are 47, 122, 44, and 131 questions. The audit
therefore covers 80 of 344 questions. Each outcome group contributes the same
number of cases, although the groups differ in size in the full test set.
Results are reported as counts within each group. Pooling them without weights
would not estimate how common legal errors are in the full test set.

Two law graduate students, identified only by anonymised codes in this paper,
assessed the cases separately, then discussed them to reach agreed labels.
Their legal training was appropriate for the bounded task of comparing the
supplied questions, statutory passages, and answer spans. The task did not ask
them to provide legal advice, determine current governing law, or represent
practitioner consensus.
Both knew that their
assessments would be used in this research and agreed to that use. No
AI-generated draft assessments were shown to them during annotation or consensus.
This concerns annotation suggestions, not the system predictions being assessed.
The forms displayed the question, two statutory passages with Act names and
titles, and their answers. The labels A/B hid which passage and answer came
from the benchmark or system. These labels did not identify the annotators. The
completed forms did not collect a separate question-only assessment. Source
identifiability judgements, about whether a question points to a unique source,
were made with the candidate passages visible. The reviewers did not search
freely across statutes.

The assessment covers source identifiability, legal relevance, whether sources
are legally equivalent, answer correctness, error materiality, and text quality.
Error materiality describes how serious an error is for the answer's legal
meaning. The assessment concerns
the archived passages supplied in the forms, not a verification of current law.
The same two reviewers recorded final labels, reasons, discussion notes, and
confirmations; there was no third adjudicator. Original ratings are retained
for agreement analysis, separately from later label revisions and consensus.
Raw agreement, Cohen's nominal $\kappa$ \citep{cohen1960agreement}, Gwet's AC1
\citep{gwet2008agreement}, and quadratic weighted materiality $\kappa$
\citep{cohen1968weighted} are descriptive measures of consistency, not tests of
legal validity. Appendix~\ref{app:human_agreement} specifies the categories,
denominators, and exclusions.

\section{Establishing the Retrieval--Reading Gap}
\label{sec:results}

Under the fixed protocol in Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}, BM25
retrieves candidate paragraphs from the 1,116 statutory contexts, and \fixedreader{}
extracts an answer from each candidate. The candidate containing the maximum
raw reader span score is selected:
\begin{align}
	\hat{c}
	&= \argmax_{c\in \mathrm{Top}_k(q)} S_{\theta}(q,c), \\
	\hat{a}
	&= a_{\theta}(q,\hat{c}).
	\label{eq:retrieve_read}
\end{align}
Here, $\mathrm{Top}_k(q)$ is the BM25-ranked candidate set, $S_{\theta}$ is
the maximum summed raw start--end logit score over valid spans and windows
for candidate $c$, and $a_{\theta}$ is the corresponding predicted answer span.
BM25 implementation details are given in Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}.

\section{Section Reference Based Candidate Filtering}
\label{sec:citation_rq}

RQ2 examines how question wording relates to retrieval on this benchmark.
RQ3 then tests how references in the questions affect retrieval.

\subsection{Section Matching Rule and Metadata Controls}
\label{sec:citation_aware}

This experiment tests section matching. Separate controls examine the roles
of passage titles and the number of candidates.

Each passage title begins ``Section~$n$: \ldots''. A regular expression extracts
the first section identifier from the question and title. It does not use
relevance labels, passage identities, or the annotated Act. No question names
its target Act, and section numbers recur across Acts. The rule returns a
string for 94.48\% of test questions. This is not a human validated accuracy
estimate.
Here, \emph{citation} means an internal statutory locator.

The exact case insensitive pattern is
\begin{quote}\small
\verb|\bsections?\s*[.\-]?\s*(\d+[A-Za-z]{0,2})\b|
\end{quote}
The system converts the first match to uppercase and compares it with the first
identifier in each title. The rule accepts letter suffixes. It does not infer
governing provisions, resolve nested locators, expand ranges or lists, or tell
a bare subsection match from a section locator. Examples and
item level disagreements appear in Appendix~\ref{app:review_checks}.

Of 325 parsed first references, 321 agree with the annotated source section.
We do not repair the four disagreements or 19 unparsed cases with source labels
(Appendix~\ref{app:review_checks}). Agreement does not prove parser accuracy,
identify the intended target, or show that the source is unique.

The filter is query driven rather than an annotation oracle: it reads the
locator written in the question and receives neither the annotated Act nor the
annotated paragraph identity. It is nevertheless a structurally privileged
condition because it assumes that an explicit section locator is present and
parsable. It is therefore evaluated as a diagnostic intervention, with title,
random-pool, wrong-locator, predicted-Act, and oracle-Act controls, rather than
as a general replacement for content retrieval.

We evaluate two structural methods. The \emph{boost} adds 1,000 BM25-score
units to passages from the parsed section and ranks the full corpus. The
\emph{filter} ranks only passages from the parsed section with BM25 on paragraph
text only. It returns all matches when fewer than five exist. If no section is
found, it falls back to unrestricted BM25. Both methods sort by descending
score and then by ascending corpus index to break ties.

Boost and filter have identical recall@1/@3/@5
(50.29/73.26/86.34). However, 73 top five sets differ because boost fills pools
smaller than five from the full corpus. The selected passage differs for 20
questions. In this subgroup, filtering adds 13.06 reader-F1 points. It adds
2.77 points overall. At rank 10, their recalls are 95.64 and 95.35. Filter uses BM25 fallback
for 19 unparsed queries.

Titles raise BM25 recall@5 from 22.67 to 56.98, while section filtering reaches
86.34. Titles within that pool add only 1.16 points
(Table~\ref{tab:metadata_controls}). The title-aware BM25 control accounts for
part of the difference between BM25 on paragraph text only and section
filtering. The stronger representation-matched comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:modern_bge_indic} reduces the remaining gap: title-plus-paragraph
fusion with BGE reranking reaches 83.72 recall@5, 2.62 points below section
filtering. Section matching therefore remains a useful diagnostic signal, but
the large paragraph-only gap should not be interpreted as evidence that a
strong retriever cannot use title metadata.
Because the benchmark contains only 19 citation-free questions, this result
should not be read as evidence that section filtering is broadly effective for
questions without statutory locators. It measures the value of an available
locator in this question set and its associated candidate pools.

For a parsed query with nonempty section pool \(C_i\), random ordering gives
expected top five source recovery
\[
p_i=\mathbf{1}\{g_i\in C_i\}\min(1,5/|C_i|),
\qquad \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{R@5}]=\frac{100}{344}\sum_{i=1}^{344}p_i.
\]
Here \(g_i\) is the annotated paragraph. For a query using fallback, \(p_i\)
is instead the fixed unrestricted BM25 top five hit indicator. Therefore, the 19
unparsed questions do not receive random full corpus rankings.
The section matched pool contains 7.89 passages on average (median 8, range
1--15). Of 325 parsed queries, 82 have at most five matches and 243 have more.
Random ordering of these pools gives an expected recall@5 of 66.72. A
10,000-order simulation gives 66.72, with SD 2.07 and a 95\% range of
$[62.79,70.93]$. BM25 within the same pool reaches 86.34. The difference
between BM25 ranking and expected random ordering is 19.62 points, with a
paired source-cluster 95\% interval of $[13.73,25.46]$. A separate size-only
control takes random pools with the same number of paragraphs from the full
corpus. It reaches only 3.86 recall@5. Thus, the section filter is not
explained by candidate-pool size alone: the matched pool makes the annotated
paragraph more likely to be available, and BM25 further ranks it within that
pool. This does not make the filter a general retrieval method; it uses a
section identifier already present in these questions.

Figure~\ref{fig:retrieval_progression} summarises this retrieval progression
and the differences between the two operational question types.

Oracle Act restriction raises BM25 recall@5 to 35.17 and Act plus section
restriction to 96.80. The 79.07 Act name control additionally modifies query
and candidate text. These are diagnostic, not deployable, comparisons.

We also tested whether a predicted Act could improve the section filter without
using annotated Act labels. A TF--IDF logistic-regression classifier used only
question text and Act labels from 3,691 training pairs. Nineteen training pairs
whose question text exactly repeated a validation or test question were removed
before fitting. The classifier was not tuned or refit on validation or test
data. It reaches 81.37\% Act accuracy on validation and 78.20\% on test.
Intersecting its predicted Act with the parsed section reduces test recall@5
from 86.34 to 75.00. If an empty intersection falls back to the section pool,
recall@5 is 77.91. The loss is consistent with an incorrect Act prediction
removing the annotated paragraph. The test result therefore does not support
adding this simple Act classifier to the section filter.

As a locator-sensitivity check, we replaced each parsed section number with a
different valid number, chosen without using the annotated Act or source. None
of the 325 parsed questions retained the annotated paragraph in the resulting
top five. The overall recall@5 is 3.49 because the 19 unparsed questions keep
the usual BM25 fallback, which supplies all 12 hits. This result is expected
for a hard section filter. It shows sensitivity to the locator, not that the
system has learned legal reasoning.

For questions in the zero-residual reference-pattern group, Act restriction changes BM25 recall@5 from 0.00
to 5.65 and section filter recall from 83.62 to 100.00. The corresponding
content-bearing values are 46.71--66.47 and 89.22--93.41. For section
filtering, the oracle Act restriction closes a larger gap for
the zero-residual reference-pattern group (16.38 points) than for content-bearing questions
(4.19 points). This pattern is consistent with greater cross Act ambiguity
when descriptive wording is absent.

\subsection{Candidate Ambiguity and Ranking Robustness}
\label{sec:candidate_ambiguity}

Using the first parsed section, which is the value used by the section-filter
system, 301 of 325 parsed queries (92.6\%) match passages from multiple Acts;
the other 24 match passages from one Act. A separate audit forms the union of
candidates for every recognised section reference in each question. Under
that broader rule, 302 questions match multiple Acts, 23 match one Act, and 19
have no recognised locator. The one-question difference is caused by a
multi-reference Copyright question whose first locator, Section 30A, occurs in
one Act, while its second locator, Section 19, occurs in several Acts. Section
matching therefore narrows
the pool without necessarily identifying the annotated Act. The retrieval
evaluation counts matches with the annotated source. It does not establish
that the system has identified the Act intended by the question.
The separate human audit examines whether the source can be identified for 80 sampled cases
with candidate evidence visible (Section~\ref{sec:human_results}); it does not
validate Act identification across the full test set.

Section filtering reduces top five annotated-source misses from 266 to 47 and
cross Act top ranked mismatches from 188 to 40. Of the 40 parsed misses, 35 rank
another Act first. Seven additional misses use BM25 fallback. Residual errors thus
mainly concern provisions from different Acts that share a section number, without establishing why.

The broader all-reference grouping is used here as a separate ambiguity audit:
it asks how many cross-Act alternatives are opened by any recognised locator in
the question. The reported section-filter outcomes still come from the system's
first parsed locator. The union-based pool-size groups therefore provide context
for ambiguity rather than reproduce the exact pool used at inference. Among the
302 multiple Act questions, section filter recall@5 is 100.00
for pools of 2--5 passages, 91.28 for pools of 6--10, and 72.16 for pools of
11 or more. The corresponding reader F1 values are 78.26, 53.82, and 45.05.
The 23 one Act questions reach 100.00 recall@5 and 88.57 reader F1. These
groups are descriptive because pool size is related to the Act and question
mix. Still, the remaining decline is concentrated in larger candidate pools
that contain provisions with the same number from several Acts.

Randomly ordering passages with equal scores also gives similar results.
Across 10,000 random orders, BM25 and section filter mean recall@5 are 22.82 (SD 0.15) and
86.30 (SD 0.18). They have only one and two boundary tie queries,
respectively.

\section{Reference Sensitivity and Redirection Stress Tests}
\label{sec:citation_ablation}

Four query variants test the reference directly: the original query, a query
with the reference removed, a reference only query, and a query with the
section number replaced by another section from the same Act. Removal cleans
dangling wording. The reference only query keeps the first section locator.
Replacement keeps the other wording and acts as an artificial redirection
stress test. It is not a model of user error. We use the annotated Act only to
draw the replacement. We never give it to the retriever.

We sample replacement sections uniformly from other sections in the annotated
Act. The unchanged clause text can make the query inconsistent. Recall remains
measured against the original annotation. We use seeds 7, 42, and 99, and the
table reports seed 42. Of 344 questions, 325 have a parsed section. The other
19 receive no reference only or replacement query, although cleanup changes
six of them. We do not retrain any model. This experiment uses a 384-token
cross-encoder limit rather than 512 tokens. Its original query scores are
therefore specific to this experiment.

The section boost system uses the 1,000-point rule defined in
Section~\ref{sec:citation_aware}. Removal triggers BM25 fallback. Replacement
changes the boosted pool by construction. Learned systems provide the
not programmed comparison.

\section{Human Evaluation of Sources and Answers}

the supplied material. It does not replace the benchmark labels or change the

often on some questions in the audit form than on others. Low agreement on

\section{External Retrieval Comparison on UK-StatuteCorpus}
\label{sec:uk_external_validation}

An external comparison tests whether the content retrieval results also appear
outside the Indian benchmark. We evaluate the original small model systems and
the modern BGE extension on UK-StatuteCorpus Eval100
\citep{alshehri2026neural}. It contains 100 English questions over 124,796 UK
statutory passages from 2020--2024. Each question has three relevant passages,
one at each relevance grade from 1 to 3. This gives 300 graded judgements. The
dataset provides passage relevance labels rather than answer spans. Therefore,
this experiment evaluates retrieval ranking only. It does not repeat the
retrieval--reading analysis. The saved run record lists the corpus,
query, model, and evaluation settings. The UK runs use depth-100 retrieval and
fusion lists, then rerank the first 50 candidates. The legacy cross-encoder and
both BGE checkpoints use a 512-token limit. The BGE extension uses the same
representation with paragraph text only and the same pinned model revisions as
Table~\ref{tab:modern_bge_indic}.
The UK queries were not processed with the Indian section parser or with the
Indian reference removal, reference only, or corrupted reference variants.
They used only the fixed system preprocessing. This includes trimmed query
strings, lowercase alphanumeric tokenisation for BM25, and the standard
``query:`` and ``passage:`` prefixes for E5. It also includes the saved BGE
checkpoint defaults, without added Act or title metadata. The separate UK
reference audit only records reference patterns. It does not change the
evaluated queries.

For UK evaluation, let \(G_i\) be all released passages with positive
relevance for query \(i\), and \(T_{ik}\) the top \(k\) retrieved passages.
Reported recall is the mean of the recall values for individual questions:
\[
\mathrm{R@}k=\frac{100}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
 \frac{|T_{ik}\cap G_i|}{|G_i|}, \qquad n=100.
\]
The leading factor converts a proportion to a percentage. The result is not a
pooled count or the share of queries with any hit. Grade-3 recall replaces
\(G_i\) by its grade-3 subset. Relevance grades enter nDCG, not ordinary
recall. IndicIPR-QA has one annotated paragraph per question, so its recall
reduces to a hit rate. These two forms of recall have different meanings.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The analysis reports retrieval, annotated source selection, and answer
extraction separately. Section filtering achieves higher retrieval scores on
this reference heavy benchmark. The title-aware BGE reranker nearly closes the
retrieval difference. This shows that the tested neural pipeline can use
section titles when they are supplied. Many selection errors remain under
both the fixed reader decoder and the independent BGE selector. Other source
selection methods may give different results.

\subsection{What Does the Benchmark Measure?}

Gold context F1 measures extraction from a supplied paragraph. It does not
measure open legal research. A locator such as ``Section 25'' is useful within
that paragraph but can refer to several Acts. The operational question split
helps examine this difference. Appendix~\ref{sec:classifier_robustness} reports
checks with different thresholds. The groups are defined by a text rule, not
by human judgements about meaning. Because the questions were generated and
curated, these operational groups may not reflect the distribution of
practitioner queries; Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses this limitation.

The final ratings accepted some answers from other sources and rejected some exact
matches, showing why benchmark agreement should not be treated as a legal
judgement. Disagreement on source uniqueness also limits how far the final
ratings can be applied beyond this sample.

\subsection{Implications for Legal Information Access}

A statutory reference is a formal way to identify a provision. Its meaning
depends on the Act and legislative structure
\citep{louisFindingLaw2023,noceJuriFindITItalian2026}. The same section number
can occur in different Acts, so matching the number alone may not identify
the intended source.

In Table~\ref{tab:qualitative}, BM25 answers a Trade Marks question from the
Patents Act. This annotation-source mismatch is not a judgement about the
controlling law. Displaying the Act and provision would make this type of
mismatch visible to a user.

\subsection{Legal Significance of Retrieval Errors}

Selecting a passage from a different Act needs closer examination. Similar
wording may hide differences in the scope, conditions, or exceptions of a
provision. However, a mismatch with the annotation alone does not prove that
the answer is legally wrong. This study does not measure harm from legal
advice or determine which provision governs a legal question. The human audit
instead illustrates why answer overlap, source identity, relevance, and the
assessed importance of errors need to be considered together in this sample.

\subsection{Implications for Statutory QA System Design}

The metadata controls show that the representation of statutory passages
affects annotated-source recovery on this benchmark. Paragraph text alone omits
useful structural signals: adding section titles improves both BM25 and the
tested neural pipeline, while section identifiers narrow the candidate pool.
BM25 ranking within the same section pool adds value beyond random ordering.
These findings do not show a general inability of strong retrievers to use
statutory structure. They motivate testing richer metadata, including the
hierarchy of Acts, sections, and clauses, relationships between sections, and
cross-references. The additional value of these untested signals remains open.

For systems that handle questions like those in this benchmark, aligning query
locators with statutory metadata may be useful. Content can then rank
provisions with the same number, while a content fallback handles questions
without a parsed locator. Checks between the selected source, locator, and
wording may catch some redirections. However, questions with too little detail may
remain unresolved. Clause level and cross-reference parsing were not tested.

\subsection{Implications for Benchmark Design}

For benchmarks with similar question forms, separate reporting of the
zero-residual reference pattern and lexically content-bearing questions may make aggregate
scores easier to interpret. Such benchmarks could also provide separate tracks
for locator resolution, content retrieval, and extraction from an identified
provision.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The findings apply to this benchmark. Searching paragraph text alone leaves
out titles that many generated questions cite. Part of the retrieval problem
therefore comes from information missing from the index. Adding titles to
the neural pipeline reduces the
recall@5 difference between BGE reranking and section filtering to 2.62 points,
and the fixed-reader comparison still depends on how the source is selected.
This is one model setting without legal training, not a general comparison of
retrievers that use titles. The question grouping rule and section parser
have not been checked through human judgements of question meaning. The
questions were generated and then curated, so the frequency of explicit
statutory references may differ from naturally occurring legal queries. Their
repeated wording may make section references especially useful. The results may therefore partly reflect
how questions were generated and filtered, rather than how users normally ask
questions. Comparisons within Acts do not fully separate these possible
effects. Validation on independently written, practitioner-generated queries,
with and without explicit references, remains an important direction for
future work; no such set is evaluated here.

The reader's raw scores are not calibrated across passages. The results
therefore depend on both the retrieved candidates and the fixed answer
selection rule. They are not an upper bound on source selection performance.
An independent BGE selector improves selection of the annotated source, but
this does not establish that its scores are calibrated or that its choices
are legally relevant. The remaining query words may reflect legal content,
headings, or the style of the question generator. Equal scores and corpus
order may also affect ranking. Dataset validation covers 200 pairs
\citep{anonymous2026indiciprqa}. The separate 80-case audit in this study
examines outputs from the section filter pipeline and their retained annotations.

The human audit involves two law graduate students and 20 cases per pipeline outcome.
It is not a practitioner study or an independent third-party adjudication.
The students saw two candidate passages when judging whether a question
identified a unique source. Their ratings do not show whether the question
alone identifies one source across the full corpus. Agreement varies across
the rating fields, especially source uniqueness and text quality. Discussion
can change labels that were originally shared as well as those that differed.
The final agreement is not an independent legal standard. The audit does not
validate the question grouping rule or section parser, compare legal adequacy
across retrieval systems, or estimate rates for all 344 questions from the
pooled sample. It also does not correct the benchmark labels or repeat the
retrieval study on independent data. A larger follow-up should include
practising lawyers or legal researchers, independent adjudication, and cases
sampled to support population-level estimates.

The same 344 questions support model selection, diagnosis, and evaluation. The
question grouping in RQ2 was developed from this test material, and the
interventions were defined before their runs but not preregistered. These
analyses characterize this fixed benchmark rather than confirm a general
relationship. The tested methods
include BM25, four small zero-shot encoders, BGE-M3, two cross-encoders, fusion,
and a rule based section reference procedure. The BGE extension is a stronger
general purpose comparison, not a legally supervised retriever. The experiments
do not cover supervised legal retrievers, late interaction, alternative
title-aware neural architectures, or matching at finer levels such as clauses.
Recall@50 limits what reranking can recover. Results close to zero for the
zero-residual reference-pattern group also limit claims
about the learned systems. Training on statutory question--provision pairs is
an important next comparison. Supervised retrievers, LLM based rerankers, or
generative QA pipelines may achieve higher scores. Evaluating them would move
the study towards improving the system rather than explaining its errors.
The purpose here is to locate errors in retrieval and source selection,
not to claim that the tested systems are the best available methods.

The UK study tests content retrieval, not the full Indian mechanism. Also,
only 19 IndicIPR-QA questions have no citation. A closer replication needs
aligned provision relevance, answer evidence, suitable models, and overlapping
locators. UK-StatuteCorpus, EQUALS, BSARD, JuriFindIT, and IL-TUR do not jointly
provide these conditions
\citep{chen2023equals,louisSpanakisBSARD2022,noceJuriFindITItalian2026,
joshiILTUR2024}. IndicIPR-QA is also limited to answerable, single section,
English questions over seven Indian IPR Acts. The results do not establish
transfer to other jurisdictions or legal fields, to case law or contracts, or
to multilingual systems. The benchmark also excludes unanswerable, multiple
section, and interactive settings. Future work should test the diagnostic
framework with LLM and RAG pipelines, independently written questions, and
other legal documents and languages. We have not
verified that the archived statutes are currently consolidated or in force.
The findings are not legal advice.

\begin{samepage}
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study examines where errors arise when a fixed reader must retrieve and
select statutory evidence before extracting an answer. On IndicIPR-QA,
paragraph-only retrieval produces a large retrieval--reading gap. Adding
section titles or matching section identifiers improves annotated-source
recovery, showing that passage representation matters alongside retrieval
model choice. However, provisions with the same number remain ambiguous across
Acts, and both the reader decoder and an independent reranker still make source
selection errors. The human audit also shows that benchmark agreement and
assessed legal adequacy are different measures. These findings describe one
benchmark and fixed pipeline: section filtering is a diagnostic intervention,
and the question-pattern analysis is exploratory. Separate reporting of
candidate retrieval, source selection, and answer extraction therefore makes
the aggregate QA score easier to interpret in this setting. Validation with
independently written and multiple-section questions remains open.

\subsection{Original Human Ratings and Final Agreement}
\label{app:human_agreement}

Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement} uses the original separate assessments:
\texttt{Frozen\_Input\_A} and \texttt{Original\_Input\_C}. The later revised C
version is kept so that changes can be traced, but it is not used to estimate
original agreement. Displayed evidence is matched to annotated and selected roles by
case ID and exact passage/answer contents. In three original C records,
identical evidence has different labels, and it is not possible to tell which
source each label refers to without guessing. The affected comparisons exclude both
uniqueness labels for LEX-050, both materiality labels for LEX-053, and both
correctness and materiality labels for LEX-060. Other judgements from these
cases remain included. This gives 79 pairs for uniqueness and correctness,

Uniqueness uses Yes/No/Unclear; relevance and correctness add Partly.
Equivalence also allows Not applicable. Materiality uses
None/Low/Moderate/High/Not applicable/Unclear, and text quality uses
None/Minor/Material/Unclear. Unclear and Not applicable are treated as
categories in nominal agreement, not missing values. AC1 uses pooled rater
category proportions and the fixed category sets in the table. The differences
between AC1 and $\kappa$, particularly for text quality, reflect different
ways of estimating chance agreement; reporting the larger coefficient does not resolve
the observed disagreements.

For materiality only, quadratic weighted $\kappa$ orders None, Low, Moderate,
and High as $0,1,2,3$, with agreement weight
$w_{ij}=1-[(i-j)/3]^2$. Pairs containing Unclear or Not applicable are excluded
from this ordinal calculation. Predicted-answer materiality has weighted
$\kappa=0.955$ on 74 pairs, after four further exclusions from the 78
role-identifiable pairs; annotated-answer materiality has weighted
$\kappa=0.954$ on all 78 pairs. The weighting gives partial credit to adjacent
categories and assumes equal category spacing. These values do not replace
the nominal coefficients or prove that the severity labels are legally valid.
No significance test or acceptance threshold is applied to these descriptive
coefficients.

The final agreed ratings form a separate record covering all 80 cases. They
include changes to cases where the original ratings agreed. Both reviewers'
confirmations, written reasons, and discussion notes are kept. The final labels
are not used to recalculate original agreement. The summary
script checks source-role mappings, reconstructs the seeded sample, recomputes
both agreement sheets, and summarises the final labels by sampling group without
changing the workbooks. This checks the calculations and their source records,
not how the review was conducted or whether its legal conclusions are correct.
